Some internet mysteries are mysteries because something genuinely unexplainable happened. This one is a mystery mostly because nobody who saw it in 1996 thought it was worth writing down properly, and by the time anyone went looking for answers, there was almost nothing left to look at.

## What actually landed in people's inboxes

On August 5, 1996, a post hit the Usenet newsgroup **alt.religion.christian.boston-church**. It wasn't spam in any recognizable commercial sense, no product, no link, no pitch. It was just words. Here's an actual surviving sample, quoted whole:

> "jitterbugging McKinley Abe break Newtonian inferring caw update Cohen air collaborate rue sportswriting rococo invocate tousle shadflower Debby Stirling pathogenesis escritoire adventitious novo ITT most chairperson Dwight Hertzog different pinpoint dunk McKinley pendant firelight Uranus episodic medicine ditty craggy flogging variac brotherhood Webb impromptu file countenance inheritance cohesion refrigerate morphine napkin inland Janeiro nameable yearbook hark."

Read it twice and the shape of the trick becomes obvious: it's not random letters, it's real English words, correctly spelled, occasionally even brushing up against a grammatical unit (a noun after an adjective, a verb where a verb belongs), and then immediately face-planting back into nonsense. It reads like a sentence that forgot what it was doing halfway through every single clause. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markovian_Parallax_Denigrate))

That post wasn't alone. Hundreds of near-identical messages went out across a wide, unrelated spread of newsgroups over the following weeks, technology boards, religion boards, hobbyist boards, nothing that shared an obvious theme. Every single one carried the exact same subject line: **"Markovian Parallax Denigrate,"** three more words that mean nothing together but which stuck hard enough to become the whole event's name. The senders attached fake, faintly absurd names to the From field, "Chris Brokerage" and "Myron Sterile" both show up in what survives, and Usenet's own architecture did the rest of the damage: posts expired off servers on their own schedule, archiving was inconsistent across providers, and by the time anyone cared enough to go digging, the overwhelming majority of the actual messages were already gone. What's left today is essentially one preserved specimen and a handful of secondhand descriptions. ([Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2024/12/12/strange-1996-markovian-parallax-denigrate-usenet-posts-remain-a-mystery.html))

## The spy who wasn't there

For years, the single most repeated "clue" in this story was a name sitting in one message's From field: **susan_lindauer**. And that name isn't nothing. A real Susan Lindauer, a former congressional press secretary, was federally indicted in March 2004 for acting as an unregistered agent of the Iraqi Intelligence Service during the run-up to the 2003 invasion, accused of accepting money from Saddam Hussein's government. Two judges later found her mentally unfit to stand trial, and the case was formally dropped in 2009. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna4506229))

Put those two facts side by side and it's easy to see why the theory caught fire: a cryptic mass-posting in 1996, tied to a name that turns out eight years later to belong to someone charged in an actual espionage case. It has the shape of something. It just isn't true.

Journalist Kevin Morris ran this down properly for the Daily Dot in 2012 and did the simplest thing anyone could have done years earlier: he contacted the real Susan Lindauer directly. She denied any connection to the posts, flatly. Digging into the actual email header, the "susan_lindauer" address traced back to an account at the University of Wisconsin–Stevens Point, belonging to a student who had nothing to do with the espionage case and had, as far as anyone could establish, no connection to the messages either. The name looks to have been harvested essentially at random and dropped into a spoofed sender field, the same way a modern spam bot might pull a plausible name out of a leaked list to fill in a header. ([Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/2018/09/16/the-usenet-markovian-parallax-denigrate-mystery)) Once you know that, the "coded message from an intelligence asset" reading falls apart completely, and what's left is a much more boring, much more probable story: whoever built these posts wasn't hiding a secret behind a real spy's name. They just grabbed a name that happened to exist.

## So who actually did it, and why

Nobody has ever been identified, but the theories that survived scrutiny cluster into three shapes.

The mundane one, and the one most people who study this land on eventually, is that this was an early, clumsy experiment in automated text generation, quite possibly built on a **Markov chain**, the same basic technique the mystery's own name accidentally advertises. A Markov chain text generator works by learning which words statistically tend to follow which other words in some source text, then stitching new sentences together one probabilistic step at a time. The output is locally plausible (each word choice makes sense given the word right before it) and globally meaningless (there's no sentence-level structure holding it together), which is an almost perfect description of the surviving sample. Spammers in the mid-to-late '90s were already experimenting with exactly this kind of text as a crude way to slip mass mailings past primitive keyword filters, on the theory that real-looking words in real-looking sentence shapes would read as legitimate to a filter that wasn't actually parsing meaning. ([Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/markovian-parallax-denigrate-mystery-internet))

The second theory is less mechanical and more personal. Cipher historian Nick Pelling, who's spent a career being professionally skeptical of exactly this kind of internet mystery, looked at the structural details and built a different profile: someone highly computer-literate, likely a member of the newsgroups being hit, who had a specific reason to target that particular set of communities, quite possibly retaliation for being harassed or banned somewhere. Pelling's case rests on the header construction itself: the From names, the subject lines, and the organization fields each look like they're drawing from separate, deliberately curated word lists rather than one single generator spitting out everything at once, which points toward someone who put real time into building this, not a five-minute prank. His estimate is that compiling lists at that scale would have taken weeks, maybe months, of prep work before the first post ever went out. ([Cipher Mysteries](https://ciphermysteries.com/2018/09/16/the-usenet-markovian-parallax-denigrate-mystery))

The third theory is the one that never actually survived contact with evidence: that this was a cipher, a coded transmission of some kind hiding a real message inside the noise. Thirty years on, nobody, not a professional cryptographer, not an obsessive hobbyist, not any of the people who've thrown statistical analysis at the surviving sample, has ever pulled a coherent hidden message out of it. That silence is itself informative. A cipher built to be broken eventually gets broken; a cipher nobody can crack after decades of real attention starts looking a lot more like it was never a cipher at all.

## The part that actually makes this interesting

Here's the detail that gives the whole story its strangest twist, and it isn't in the gibberish. In 1996, this event barely registered. It wasn't treated as a notable mystery by the Usenet community at the time; it looked like spam, got dismissed as spam by the people who saw it, and mostly got forgotten. The "internet's oldest unsolved mystery" framing didn't really exist until 2012, when a Daily Dot article told the story to a new generation of readers who had never been anywhere near Usenet in the first place, and that piece is also the one that ran down and killed the Susan Lindauer theory in the same breath it helped popularize the mystery. Since then, the story has been recycled through blog posts, forum threads, and a small pile of YouTube videos, each pass adding a little more "unsolved mystery" gloss to something that, at the time, most people just scrolled past. ([AV Club](https://www.avclub.com/need-a-distraction-help-solve-the-internets-oldest-mys-1842292578))

That's not a knock on the story. It's the actual point of it. Markovian Parallax Denigrate is less a message nobody could decode and more a mirror for how the internet manufactures its own folklore: a strange artifact, a debunked but too-good-to-fully-let-go theory, and thirty years of retelling that turned a probable spam-filter experiment into a legend with its own name. The text itself was never the mystery. What people did with the text afterward is.
