Every blockchain transaction is a permanent, public record. That single fact is the reason cryptocurrency investigations work at all, and the reason criminals spend so much effort trying to break the link between where funds started and where they end up.

## The shape of crypto crime

Illicit crypto activity sorts cleanly into a handful of categories: theft (hacks, exploits, robbery), fraud and scams (investment fraud, exit scams, phishing, impersonation), illicit payments (extortion, sanctions evasion, terrorist financing), and illicit commerce (darknet markets, stolen-goods trafficking). Almost all of it eventually needs the same three-stage laundering process traditional finance already has names for: placement (moving funds away from the crime), layering (breaking the trail through multiple transfers), integration (reintroducing the funds looking legitimate), just executed with blockchain-native tools instead of shell companies and cash mules.

Wallet drainers are a clean illustration of the theft side end-to-end: a phishing page or a typosquatted domain gets a victim to connect their wallet, a script embedded in the page collects the connection, the victim approves what looks like a routine transaction, and the approval is actually a transfer that empties the wallet straight to the attacker. The entire attack lives in that one approval click, which is exactly why "always verify the domain before connecting a wallet" is the standard advice it is.

## Following the money

The public ledger is the whole investigation's foundation: anyone can query it through a block explorer (Etherscan for Ethereum, Xmrchain for Monero, Blockchair across several chains) and see every transaction a given address has ever made. Raw data like that has no context on its own; turning a wallet address into something useful runs through the same pipeline every intelligence discipline uses: data becomes information once it's linked to context (this cluster belongs to this known entity), and information becomes intelligence once it's been analyzed into something actionable.

Getting from an address to an entity is clustering: grouping wallets believed to be controlled by the same actor, based on heuristics like common-input-ownership (addresses spent together in one transaction are very likely controlled by one wallet), consistent change-address behavior, and known-entity tagging. Tools like Chainalysis Reactor build this automatically, and commercial platforms like Arkham Intelligence go further: attaching real identities to clusters and tracking portfolios in real time (their public dashboard, for instance, tracks well over $90 million in wallets attributed to the Lazarus Group). Once a cluster exists, exposure analysis asks how close it sits to known illicit activity: direct exposure is funds moving straight from a flagged cluster with no intermediary; indirect exposure is the same funds having passed through one or more clusters in between, a weaker but still meaningful signal.

Individual wallets carry their own fingerprint, too. Transaction-level features (whether an address uses SegWit, whether it flags Replace-By-Fee, multisig configuration, protocol version, locktime) differ by wallet software and usage pattern, and a consistent combination of them across otherwise-unlinked addresses is a real behavioral tell, the same idea as a JA3 hash fingerprinting a TLS client.

## Breaking the trail

Every obfuscation technique is trying to do one thing: sever the on-chain link between a fund's origin and its destination. **Mixing** services pool funds from many sources and pay back out from the shared pool rather than from any one source: ChipMixer, before it was taken down, implemented this with a chip system: deposits get broken into fixed denominations that double (0.001, 0.002, 0.004 BTC, and so on), so a deposit of 0.112 BTC comes back as a combination like 0.064 + 0.032 + 0.016, deliberately making deposit and withdrawal amounts hard to match. **Peel chains** move funds through a long sequence of addresses, peeling off a small spend at each hop while the remainder carries forward: Wasabi Wallet's mixing activity around the 2016 Bitfinex theft is a well-documented public example of the pattern. **Chain hopping** swaps between different cryptocurrencies and networks (Bitcoin to Ethereum to Tether and back) to break the continuity a single-chain investigation depends on. **CoinJoin** combines multiple people's payments into one transaction so that matching a specific input to a specific output becomes genuinely ambiguous. And **privacy coins** bake obfuscation into the protocol itself: Monero's ring signatures and stealth addresses make every user anonymous by default, which is why it's usually described as the hardest cryptocurrency to trace, while Zcash makes the same privacy optional through shielded transactions.

Off-ramping adds another layer: OTC brokers move large sums directly between buyer and seller outside an exchange's order book, and many operate as **nested services**: using another platform's own hosted addresses and liquidity rather than running independent infrastructure. Suex, a Moscow-based OTC broker, was sanctioned by the US Treasury's OFAC in 2021 specifically for laundering ransomware proceeds through exactly this kind of nested arrangement inside a larger exchange.

## When the trail survives anyway

None of this is airtight, and several of the best-known cases in crypto forensics are exactly the obfuscation techniques above failing in practice.

DeadBolt ransomware delivered its decryption keys through a Bitcoin transaction's OP_RETURN field (a way to embed arbitrary data on-chain) sent to the same address the victim paid. The mechanism assumed the key would only be revealed after payment cleared, but the Dutch National Police, working with the security firm Responders.NU and Europol, exploited the timing: fake a payment, wait for DeadBolt's automated system to broadcast the key transaction, then use Replace-By-Fee to substitute the pending payment with one that returned the funds to the police instead of confirming to the attacker. The technique recovered decryption keys for roughly 90% of the victims who came forward, at close to zero cost. ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/police-tricks-deadbolt-ransomware-out-of-155-decryption-keys/), [Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/deadbolt-ransomware-strain-tricked-into-giving-up-decryption-keys/))

ChipMixer itself didn't survive either. In March 2023, German and US authorities (working with Europol, Belgium, Poland and Switzerland) seized its infrastructure: four servers, seven terabytes of data, and roughly $46 million in Bitcoin. By the DOJ's accounting, the service had laundered upward of $3 billion since 2017, including funds tied to ransomware groups and North Korean state-linked hackers. ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2023/03/15/germany-and-us-seize-over-46m-crypto-tied-to-chipmixer-investigation-europol))

And chain hopping through exchanges doesn't guarantee anonymity for the people on the other end, either. Tian Yinyin and Li Jiadong, two Chinese nationals, were indicted by the DOJ and sanctioned by OFAC for laundering more than $100 million stolen from a cryptocurrency exchange hack attributed to the Lazarus Group; investigators traced the funds through the exact kind of exchange-hopping and layering this piece describes, and it still ended in named individuals facing US charges. ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/two-chinese-nationals-charged-laundering-over-100-million-cryptocurrency-exchange-hack), [Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sm924))

## Three altitudes of intelligence

The same body of evidence supports three different kinds of intelligence product, depending on the question being asked. **Tactical** intelligence profiles one specific cluster: mapping a Diamond Model around a single ransomware developer, the bulletproof hosting provider behind their infrastructure, and the mixer they laundered proceeds through gives an immediately actionable picture of one operation. **Operational** intelligence tracks how a group's tradecraft evolves over time: North Korean state-linked actors, for instance, have a well-documented pattern of compromising an exchange through spear-phishing or trojanized trading apps, laundering the proceeds through chain hopping and mixers, and cashing out through foreign OTC brokers, and recognizing that pattern is what lets an exchange flag a DPRK-linked address before the funds move further. **Strategic** intelligence rolls activity up into trends that inform policy and resourcing: Chainalysis's on-chain tracking showed ransomware payments falling from roughly $766 million in 2021 to $457 million in 2022, a decline corroborated off-chain by incident-response firm Coveware's finding that the share of victims actually paying dropped from 70% in 2020 to 41% in 2022, two independent methodologies agreeing that ransomware profitability was genuinely declining, not just less visible. ([Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/crypto-ransomware-revenue-down-as-victims-refuse-to-pay/))

Three altitudes, one underlying fact making all of them possible: the ledger doesn't forget.
