Some mysteries show up once and vanish. This one has shown up on the same day, in the same newspaper, every single year since before most of the people investigating it were born, and the person placing it is not a mystery at all. He has a name, a law degree, and a phone number the newspaper still calls.

## The same page, every May 1st, since 1981

Since at least 1981, a full-page advertisement has run in the **Arizona Daily Wildcat**, the student newspaper at the University of Arizona, on **May 1st**, without fail. Each one is a dense collage: mathematical equations, fragments of jumbled maps, portraits, text in multiple languages, quotes lifted from places like Moby Dick and Macbeth, imagery borrowed from postage stamps and dollar bills, and, running through decades of otherwise wildly different content, a recurring cartoon drawing of a smiling, faintly smirking man with hair rendered as a handful of simple straight lines, a detail that shifts subtly from year to year but never quite disappears. ([Deseret Magazine](https://www.deseret.com/magazine/2022/11/10/23404444/may-day-mystery-true-crime-arizona-campus-paper/))

Nobody buys ad space like that by accident, and nobody keeps buying it, in the same paper, on the same day, for 44 straight years, without a reason. The group behind it calls itself **The Orphanage**. What that name means, what the ads actually communicate, and who specifically is inside it are all still open questions.

## It's older than the first ad anyone can point to

The earliest confirmed ad is from 1981, but the story behind it goes back further. According to the man who's placed the ads for decades, the actual origin sits in the middle of 1969, at the height of Vietnam-era campus unrest, with a founding motivation he's described as **"total social/theological transformation, incipit: 8/30/69."** Earlier messages did exist before 1981, in a different medium he's declined to specify, and the shift to a formal newspaper ad is generally read as the moment the project became something closer to a tradition than a one-off statement. He's also suggested that anything more explicit than what actually ran risked members' arrest, which lines up with a persistent theory that this started as something genuinely political, born directly out of the unrest of that era rather than as a puzzle built for its own sake. ([Phoenix Magazine](https://www.phoenixmag.com/2017/05/01/arizona-s-da-vinci-code/))

## The archivist who never stopped

**Bryan Hance** found his first May Day ad in 1995, as a University of Arizona freshman, and never really put it down. He stayed in Tucson after graduating specifically to keep tracking it, and in 1997 built **maydaymystery.org**, a running archive of every ad anyone can locate, going back to the start. Decades in, Hance corresponds directly with people identifying themselves as part of The Orphanage, and after this much time studying it up close, he's landed somewhere unusual for an obsessive: he doesn't think it's solvable, and he seems mostly at peace with that. ([maydaymystery.com](https://maydaymystery.com/index.php?title=Robert_Truman_Hungerford))

He's not the only one who ended up organizing a community around it. **Kate Vesely**, a Tucson native and fellow UA student, got just as pulled in, and in 2009 co-founded a Facebook group called **Mayday Mystery Fans** specifically to crowdsource theories with other people obsessing over the same images. Her own read on it: a "collective of brilliant geniuses" who formed something like an intellectual fraternity sometime in the 1970s, still operating on the same wavelength decades later.

## The lawyer who answers, eventually

The ad doesn't place itself. Every year, a Tucson attorney named **Robert Truman Hungerford** calls the paper to arrange it. Hungerford studied philosophy at the University of Arizona in the late 1960s, later earned a law degree, and by his own account speaks eight languages, including Latin, Hebrew, Russian, and Greek, which tracks with just how many different tongues and reference points show up scattered across the ads themselves.

What Hungerford actually *is* to The Orphanage depends on which source you read. Some accounts describe him strictly as outside counsel, the lawyer executing an unnamed client's instructions and nothing more. Others describe him as a member of the group itself, present at its founding as a college senior in 1969. Hungerford has never fully resolved that ambiguity in public, and it's not obviously an accident that he hasn't.

In 2013, Vesely worked up the nerve to leave a note at his downtown Tucson office. He wrote back the next day, and the two have corresponded ever since, without ever meeting face to face. Pressed on motive, Hungerford has offered lines that sound less like an explanation than an invitation: *"Like a tuning fork, there is resonance with the right people."* Asked what an eventual resolution might look like, he's said it would be *"a dramatic transformation, beyond any sea change,"* something you'd apparently have to live through to actually understand. ([Deseret Magazine](https://www.deseret.com/magazine/2022/11/10/23404444/may-day-mystery-true-crime-arizona-campus-paper/))

## Every theory that's been thrown at it

Over more than two decades of organized amateur investigation, the working theories have ranged from the plausible to the unhinged: a private intellectual fraternity using a student newspaper as a closed channel between members, an elaborate long-running piece of performance art or alternate-reality game, a genuine secret society with roots in Vietnam-era political organizing, or, more cynically, Hungerford simply running the whole thing solo under the cover of a group that may not really exist, a theory he denies. One researcher spent real effort trying to sell Hance on a connection to the **Zodiac Killer**, which Hance firmly and immediately rejected; it's the kind of theory that shows up around any long-running unsolved cipher sooner or later, purely because unsolved ciphers attract that comparison by reflex, not because the evidence supports it. ([Deseret Magazine](https://www.deseret.com/magazine/2022/11/10/23404444/may-day-mystery-true-crime-arizona-campus-paper/))

The investigation has occasionally gone further than anyone should probably admit to. At least one deeply committed investigator was arrested for going through Hungerford's actual garbage, looking for anything that might connect back to the ads. That's the kind of detail that tells you more about how this mystery has been chased than any of the ciphers themselves do: four decades in, people are still willing to go through a stranger's trash over a newspaper ad nobody's been able to read.

## It's still going, and it's not staying small

This isn't a cold case anyone's forgotten about. Each new ad now draws attention across podcasts, Reddit threads, YouTube breakdowns, TikTok, and the original Facebook group, and the 2022 ad alone picked up close to 200 comments on Hance's own site within two weeks of running. Hungerford has hinted there's "much more forthcoming," which is about as close as he's ever come to promising the tradition isn't stopping anytime soon. ([Tucson Local Media](https://www.tucsonlocalmedia.com/import/the-great-may-day-mystery-mysterious-ads-placed-in-college-newspaper-for-more-than-20/article_5daaf45c-2760-5906-b0ee-c2229127472e.html))

Neither Hance nor Vesely, the two people who've spent the most sustained effort on this of anyone alive, expects it to ever actually be solved. Vesely has made a kind of peace with that directly, describing it as "my own little mystery right here at home," tucked inside everything else in life nobody fully understands either. That's a genuinely rare note for a mystery this size to land on: not frustration that the answer's been withheld for 44 years, but something closer to gratitude that it still hasn't been given away.
