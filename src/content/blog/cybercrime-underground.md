Every intrusion breaks down into four things: an **adversary** doing it, a **capability** they're using, **infrastructure** they're running it on, and a **victim** on the other end. That's the Diamond Model of Intrusion Analysis, and it's a genuinely useful lens for the cybercrime underground specifically, because the underground is not one thing: it's forums, markets, leak sites and messaging apps, each running its own internal economy of trust, and the model gives you a fixed set of questions to ask no matter which one you're looking at.

## Where it actually lives

The starting question for any unfamiliar URL is simple: how is it accessed, what happens there, and what kind of place is it? Clearnet or darknet, discussion or trade, forum or market or leak site: that three-step sort is most of the work of orienting yourself in a new part of the underground.

**Forums** are the social layer: people visit them to learn from each other and to buy and sell, organized loosely into hacking, malware, stolen data and fraud. Profiling one comes down to a handful of attributes: who administers it (the "Administrator" tag on a profile), how old it is (registration date, or the first post after walking thread IDs backward), what gets discussed, what languages the userbase writes in, and whether making a purchase requires navigating to an escrow or guarantor system. **BreachForums** is the clean case study: founded by "Pompompurin" (Conor Brian Fitzpatrick, arrested by the FBI in March 2023) as RaidForums' direct successor after RaidForums itself was seized in [February 2022](https://therecord.media/breachforums-seized-by-fbi-months-after-arrest-of-alleged-administrator-dark-web-marketplace). It ran a credits economy: contribute and earn access to hidden content, or pay for a "God Tier" account that skips the requirement entirely: trust and money both bought the same door.

**Markets** are the retail layer, almost always Tor-hosted, running an admin/moderator/developer structure over a cryptocurrency escrow. The pattern repeats across categories: Silk Road was the first to pair Tor with Bitcoin; AlphaBay took its place as the largest after Silk Road's seizure; Hydra turned the model into a money-laundering service on top of being a drug market. On the cybercrime side, xDedic pioneered brute-forced RDP access as a commodity, Joker's Stash grew into the largest carding market in darknet history, and 2easy ("ss") and Russian Market did the same for infostealer logs. The escrow mechanic underneath all of them is the same reversible cycle: buyer deposits into the market's escrow, vendor fulfills, buyer confirms, funds release, market takes a cut, the whole point being that trust sits with the market admins, not with a stranger on the other end of a trade, which is exactly why buyers and vendors alike stay exposed to a market simply vanishing with everyone's balance.

**Leak sites and paste sites** are the disclosure layer. Leak sites range from ransomware double-extortion pages (the Grief gang's Tor site is a standard example) to explicitly political ones like WikiLeaks or DDoSecrets. Paste sites are older and blander by comparison, Pastebin (2002) is still one of the most popular, but purpose-built ones like Doxbin exist specifically to host personal information on named targets, which is where "paste site" and "doxxing" become the same activity.

**Messaging** is the operational security layer. Cybercriminals gravitate to Telegram, Discord, TOX and Jabber specifically for end-to-end encryption, Tor-friendliness and minimal registration friction, and forum threads routinely end with an offer to move a deal to a private one-on-one channel. Where phone numbers are required, eSIMs registered through foreign carriers sidestep the need to be physically present. Law enforcement has run the same game in reverse: [Operation Trojan Shield](https://www.npr.org/2021/06/08/1004332551/drug-rings-platform-operation-trojan-shield-anom-operation-greenlight) built ANOM, a "hardened" encrypted phone marketed to criminal networks as the next Phantom Secure, except every message was blind-copied to an FBI-run server the whole time. By the time the operation went public it had reached roughly 12,000 devices and 9,500 active users worldwide, and led to some 1,200 arrests.

## What gets sold

Crimeware breaks into recognizable families: banking trojans, infostealers, loaders and droppers, web shells, crypto miners, ransomware, and POS/ATM/JS skimmers, but the more useful picture is how they connect into a supply chain rather than the list itself. An infostealer, for instance, moves through five separate parties before a single credential gets used: a developer sells the code on a forum, a distributor buys it and pushes it to victims off-forum, the stolen credentials get exfiltrated back through the same off-forum channel, and then those credentials go up for sale on-forum again to a completely different buyer. Nobody in that chain necessarily meets anyone else in it.

Ransomware has gone through the same kind of evolution, from the 1989 AIDS Trojan (distributed on floppy disk) through human-operated attacks, to wormable automated spread (WannaCry), to Ransomware-as-a-Service platforms an operator builds and licenses out to affiliates (GandCrab), to today's big-game-hunting operations aimed squarely at large organizations for large ransoms (Conti). Forums sit in the middle of that entire economy: malware developers and distributors advertise services there, initial access brokers sell their way in, RaaS operators recruit affiliates, and money launderers cash the proceeds back out: the forum is less a marketplace for ransomware specifically than the labor exchange the whole business runs on.

Commercial offensive-security tooling is its own supply problem. A tool built and sold legitimately for red teams gets cracked and redistributed free on forums, at which point every advantage that made it valuable to a red team (reliability, evasion, active development) is now available to whoever downloads it. [Brute Ratel C4](https://www.sans.org/blog/cracked-brute-ratel-c4-framework-proliferates-across-the-cybercriminal-underground), a legitimate adversary-simulation platform built by Chetan Nayak, is the clean example: a cracked copy surfaced on VirusTotal in September 2022, spread through private Telegram groups, and within weeks was circulating openly across BreachForums, CryptBB, RAMP, Exploit.in and XSS.is. And a forum ad selling a piece of malware is, read carefully, an ATT&CK mapping exercise: a rental listing that promises to "launch exe/dll in memory," run via "admin rights," execute "ps/cmd commands in memory" through "system process (rundll32/regsvr32)," delivered as "DLL/OCX/CPL," maps cleanly onto process injection, valid accounts abuse, PowerShell and command-shell execution, and binary proxy execution; the seller is describing capability in plain language, and the TTPs are sitting right there in the copy.

## What it runs on

Infrastructure is the part of an operation an adversary actually has to stand up and pay for, and it comes in three tiers with a genuinely useful property: **atomic** indicators (an IP, a domain) are trivial to burn and replace; **computed** indicators (a file hash, a domain-generation algorithm, a server fingerprint) cost more to change; **behavioral** indicators (a consistent domain naming theme, a habit of hosting C2 only on Bitcoin-accepting VPS providers, routing all interactivity through Tor exit nodes) are effectively a fingerprint of the operator's own habits, and the hardest of the three to simply swap out. That ordering, low cost to high cost, is exactly why behavioral indicators are worth more to an investigator than another IP block: they don't change when the infrastructure does.

Real infrastructure maps look like small supply chains of their own. Hydra Market ran on Mullvad for VPN, BitLaunch for VPS hosting, Bearhost for bulletproof hosting, and NameCheap for domain registration, and the same providers that served the market's admin also served completely separate criminal customers, from TrickBot's operators to a Raid Forums admin, all paying in Bitcoin for the same handful of services. Infrastructure, unlike a forum handle, tends to be reused, which is exactly what makes it traceable.

## Who's behind it

Incident response firms track cybercriminals as "groups," but that abstraction breaks down fast once you're looking at forums directly: the same person sells services to whoever's paying, and the commoditization of tooling means a "group" is often just a temporary alliance of independently-operating specialists: developers (Evgeniy Bogachev, the ZeuS banking trojan's author, is the canonical example), operators, administrators, money launderers, and infrastructure-as-a-service providers, each of whom could be working with three other "groups" simultaneously.

Reputation is the currency that holds those temporary alliances together. Profile pages carry reviews left by counterparties, forums with dispute-resolution sections keep a visible arbitration history, and vouching between two established sellers is a real, checkable signal of a working relationship. For a prolific actor, that accumulated reputation is worth defending in its own right: it's collateral, in a marketplace with no courts.

## Who pays for it

Not every attack produces a victim (a target only becomes a victim once the attack actually succeeds), and finding out who did depends heavily on what kind of compromise you're chasing. Breach victims surface through data brokers advertising databases on forums, marketplace listings, file-sharing sites, and dedicated breach-search engines like Have I Been Pwned, DeHashed and SpyCloud. Malware infections leave a different trail: bulk logs dumped or sold on forums and markets, netflow between a C2 server and a victim network, open directories exposed by Google dorking, credentials landing on unsecured FTP servers, or victims unwittingly uploading their own infection to a public sandbox. Ransomware victims are usually easiest to enumerate, since the data-leak-site model makes the disclosure public by design, supplemented by news reporting, sandbox submissions, and internet-wide scanning.

That data supports real pattern analysis. Pulling data-leak-site listings into a spreadsheet and charting DarkSide, BlackMatter and BlackCat victim counts over time doesn't show three separate groups: it shows [one group rebranding twice](https://www.security.com/threat-intelligence/noberus-blackcat-ransomware-ttps), tracked back to the same actor (FIN7, also known as Carbanak or Carbon Spider) each time law-enforcement pressure or a high-profile incident (DarkSide's, after the Colonial Pipeline attack) made the old name too hot to keep using. Purpose-built OSINT tools exist for exactly this kind of tracking: ID-Ransomware fingerprints a ransom note against a known-family database, Ransomware.live and Malpedia track victims and campaigns respectively, and MalwareBazaar and VirusTotal hold queryable samples wired into automated sandboxes for anyone who needs to detonate a binary rather than just read about it.

None of these layers exist in isolation. A forum sells access to a market; a market's escrow protects a crimeware sale; the crimeware runs on rented infrastructure; the infrastructure compromises a victim; the victim's data gets sold back through the same forum it started on. The Diamond Model isn't describing four separate things: it's describing four vantage points on one continuous cycle.
