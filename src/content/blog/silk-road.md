Okay so imagine you're 26, you've got a physics degree, you're really into libertarian economics, and your big idea is: what if there was an Amazon, but for drugs, and nobody could ever get caught buying or selling on it? That's not a hypothetical. That's literally how Silk Road happened, and the guy who thought it up is named Ross Ulbricht.

## The origin story

Ulbricht launched Silk Road in January 2011, running it on Tor (so nobody could trace where the site actually lived) and settling exclusively on Bitcoin for payment (so nobody could trace the money either). He named it after the actual historic Silk Road — the old trade network connecting Asia and Europe — which, fine, is a genuinely good name for a marketplace, even if this one mostly moved weed and MDMA instead of silk and spices.

He ran the whole thing under a pseudonym: **Dread Pirate Roberts**, or **DPR** for short, lifted straight from *The Princess Bride* — where "Dread Pirate Roberts" is a title that gets passed down from one person to the next, not one guy's actual identity. Cute reference. Also, weirdly, kind of foreshadowing, because DPR did eventually become a shared identity of sorts — more on that in a second.

## How big did this thing actually get

Big. Genuinely, absurdly big. By the time it got taken down, Silk Road had processed something like **$1.2 billion** in total sales, run through **more than 100,000 buyers**, moved hundreds of kilograms of drugs from thousands of vendors, and — because why stop at drugs — also had listings for computer hacking services, forged documents, and (allegedly) hitmen. Ulbricht's cut was a straightforward commission on every sale, and that commission alone added up to **roughly 600,000 Bitcoin**, worth about $80 million at the time. ([NPR](https://npr.org/2025/01/21/nx-s1-5270051/trump-pardons-dark-web-marketplace-creator-ross-ulbricht), [ICE](https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/hsi-seizes-biggest-anonymous-drug-black-market-website-and-assists-arrest-operator))

He didn't run it alone, either — nobody runs a multi-million-dollar business solo, turns out, even a felony one. Early on he brought in a mentor figure who went by **Variety Jones** (later "Cimon"), who basically became DPR's advisor on everything from security to business strategy. Below him were moderators and admins handling the actual day-to-day grind: customer service, resolving disputes between buyers and sellers who — shockingly — didn't always have great mechanisms for trust when one party was, again, a drug dealer. One of these staffers, going by the handle **ChronicPain** (real name Curtis Green), is about to become extremely relevant.

## The screwup that ended it all

Here's the thing about running a billion-dollar criminal enterprise: you only have to mess up once. And Ulbricht's one mess-up was almost comically low-tech.

Way back near the very start, before Silk Road was Silk Road-famous, someone using the handle **"Altoid"** posted about the site on a couple of forums — Bitcointalk and a mushroom-growing forum called Shroomery — basically doing early marketing for his own creation. Fine, whatever, lots of founders hype their own stuff. The problem is that months later, on Bitcointalk again, "Altoid" posted looking for an "IT pro" to help with a Bitcoin startup — and told people to email their applications to **rossulbricht@gmail.com**. His actual, real, full name Gmail address. Federal investigators eventually connected those dots, and that was basically game over. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/end-silk-road-slip-ups-building-internet-drug-market-led-flna8c11326181))

The arrest itself is a genuinely great story. FBI agents cornered Ulbricht in October 2013 at the **science fiction section of a public library in San Francisco** — specifically because they needed to grab his laptop *while it was open and he was logged in*. Why? Because the whole laptop was encrypted, and if he'd had even a second to slam it shut, everything on it would've been useless to them. So the arrest was staged as a distraction — agents created a fake commotion to get him to step away from the keyboard, then snatched the laptop before he could touch it. Logged in as Dread Pirate Roberts, in real time, with a Bitcoin wallet on the machine holding roughly **144,000 Bitcoin** — worth $16–18 million at 2013 prices, and, per FBI forensic testimony, with about 700,000 BTC having passed through that same wallet from Silk Road's own servers over the preceding year. ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1626194/fbi-consultant-silk-road-founder-had-16-18m-worth-of-bitcoins-on-laptop.html))

## The part where it gets genuinely unhinged

So this is where the story stops being a normal true-crime arc and starts being *insane*.

Prosecutors alleged that DPR solicited a total of **six** murders-for-hire over the site's run, paying out roughly $650,000 in Bitcoin combined. The two best-documented: $80,000 to have Curtis Green (ChronicPain) killed after Green got busted separately and DPR worried he'd flip, and $150,000 to a user called "redandwhite" to kill someone using the handle "FriendlyChemist" who was allegedly trying to blackmail him. Here's the twist inside the twist: the Curtis Green "hit" was actually a DEA sting the whole time — Green wasn't hurt, the whole thing was staged with his cooperation, and DPR seemingly had no idea. And across every single one of the six alleged plots, investigators never found evidence any murder actually happened. Ulbricht was never formally charged or tried on the murder-for-hire counts in the case that convicted him — they surfaced as allegations and sentencing evidence (his own journal, chat logs) rather than counts a jury ruled on, and his supporters have disputed the whole narrative pretty hard ever since. Genuinely contested history, worth knowing that going in. ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/silk-road-drug-vendor-who-claimed-commit-murders-hire-silk-road-founder-ross-ulbricht))

But wait, it gets weirder, because it turns out *the government's own investigators* were also dirty. Two federal agents who worked the Silk Road task force — DEA agent **Carl Force** and Secret Service agent **Shaun Bridges** — were secretly stealing from the investigation the entire time. Force created fake online personas (including one hilariously named "French Maid") to sell inside information back to DPR for around $100,000 in Bitcoin, on top of straight-up extorting him. Bridges, separately, just stole about **$820,000 worth of Bitcoin** during the probe, and then — not content with one theft — stole another **1,606 Bitcoin** from an unrelated government seizure later. Both pled guilty. Force got six and a half years, Bridges got just under six. The people investigating the biggest drug marketplace on the internet were robbing it on the side. You cannot make this up. ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/former-secret-service-agent-sentenced-scheme-related-silk-road-investigation))

## The fall, and the almost-immediate sequel nobody asked for

Ulbricht was convicted in February 2015 on all seven counts — narcotics trafficking conspiracy, computer hacking conspiracy, money laundering conspiracy, and running a continuing criminal enterprise, which is the same "kingpin" statute usually reserved for cartel bosses. He got sentenced in May 2015 to **two life sentences without parole, plus 40 years**, on top of having to forfeit $183 million. About as final an ending as the justice system hands out.

Except Silk Road itself didn't stay dead. **Five weeks** after the original site got seized, "Silk Road 2.0" popped up, eventually run by a guy going by "Defcon" — real name Blake Benthall, a former SpaceX engineer, because apparently that's a talent pool that produces darknet market operators too. It lasted about a year — reportedly hitting 150,000 active users and $8 million in monthly sales before it fell — before an undercover Homeland Security agent worked their way into admin-level trust on the site and the FBI shut it down in November 2014, arresting Benthall in San Francisco. Silk Road as a brand was just done after that. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/security/fbi-arrests-alleged-silk-road-2-0-operator-blake-benthall-n242751))

## The plot twist absolutely nobody predicted

And then, twelve years after his arrest — this actually happened, this isn't a hypothetical "what if" ending — **Donald Trump gave Ross Ulbricht a full, unconditional presidential pardon**, on January 21, 2025. Wiped the record completely clean. It fulfilled a promise Trump had made to Libertarian Party supporters during his 2024 campaign, where Ulbricht had become something of a cause célèbre in libertarian circles as a symbol of harsh federal sentencing. Trump announced it on Truth Social by saying he'd personally called Ulbricht's mother to tell her. Two consecutive life sentences, commuted to a pardon, by a phone call. ([NPR](https://npr.org/2025/01/21/nx-s1-5270051/trump-pardons-dark-web-marketplace-creator-ross-ulbricht))

So that's Silk Road: started by one guy with a Tor browser and a grudge against centralized authority, grew into a billion-dollar black market, got taken down by a Gmail address and a library ambush, involved not one but two corrupt federal agents actively robbing the case they were building, produced murder-for-hire allegations that were never actually proven, spawned a sequel that lasted about a year, and ended — after two life sentences — with a pardon. If you pitched this as fiction, an editor would tell you to cut the ending. Reality didn't care.
